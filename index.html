Background

Convection is a fundamental process in natural fluid systems: hot material rises, cool material sinks, and large-scale overturning currents develop that redistribute heat and momentum. These motions drive atmospheric circulation and ocean currents on Earth, mix the molten interiors of planets, and facilitate energy transport in stellar interiors. Understanding convection is essential for building models of planetary and stellar structure, and it also provides the fluid motions that can give rise to magnetic dynamos.

Problem

Convection simulations are notoriously computationally expensive. Consider the simplified Rayleigh–Bénard convection system given by \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 \] \[\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u} = - \nabla p + T \mathbf{\hat{z}} + \sqrt{\frac{\Pr}{\rm{Ra}}} \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} \] \[\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Pr \rm{Ra}}} \nabla^2 T \] where \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( T \) respectively denote the fluid's velocity and temperature. The Rayleigh number (Ra) sets the convective strength while the Prandtl number (Pr) is an intrinsic property of a given fluid. In astrophysical and geophysical systems, Rayleigh numbers often exceed \(10^{20}\) whereas direct numerical simulations (DNS) of the Rayleigh–Bénard system can not exceed \( \rm{Ra}\sim10^{12} \) due to resolution requirements. To illustrate this difficulty, here's a simulation by the Dedalus group run at \( \rm{Ra}=10^{11}\). My goal for this project was to develop a reduced model for Rayleigh–Bénard convection which could be implemented at high Rayleigh number while avoiding intensive computation.

Solution

We can reduce the dimensionality of (and thereby simplify) the convection problem by making the quasilinear approximation. To construct a quasilinear model, consider the temperature's horizontal average \( \overline{T}(z) \) which only depends on the vertical coordinate \( z \). The original temperature field \( T \) can then be expressed as \( T(x, z, t) = \overline{T}(z) + T'(x, z, t) \) where \( T' \) is called the temperature perturbation. Next, we approximate the temperature and velocity perturbations (\( T' \) and \( \mathbf{u}' \)) by solving a linear eigenvalue problem involving \( \overline{T} \). To approximate an equilibrated convective system, we apply the "marginal stability" constraint such that the maximum eigenvalue \( \rm{max}\{ \sigma \} = 0 \). Finally, we iteratively update \( \overline{T} \) according to a quasilinear equation involving the linear perturbations (\( T' \) and \( \mathbf{u}' \)) until convergence. The converged \( \overline{T} \) is called a Marginally Stable Thermal Equilibrium (MSTE).

Background

Even when the partial differential equation underlying a physical process can be evolved forward in time, the retrospective (backward in time) inverse problem often has its own challenges and applications. Here are a few significant examples of previous works focused on solving retrospective inverse problems:

[1] precisely localized tumor initiation sites through the application of biophysical constraints.
[2] developed an algorithm to determine the initial state of a supernova.
[3] investigated the history of buoyancy-driven mantle flows by reconstructing past temperature distributions.

Although there are problem-specific details in their methodologies, [1-3] use the same optimal control technique known as Direct Adjoint Looping (DAL).

Problem

Consider a retrospective inverse problem where the variable of interest \(\overline{X}\) is constrained by a partial differential equation (PDE) involving space \( x \) and time \( t \): \begin{align} \mathcal{F}[\overline{X}(x, t)] = 0. \end{align} We aim to determine the unique initial condition \( \overline{X}(x, 0)\) corresponding to a given final state \(\overline{X}(x, t_f)\) where the final time \( t_f \) is known. In this work, we focus on advective PDEs such as Korteweg–de Vries–Burgers (KdVB) and incompressible Navier–Stokes. Our goal is to assess (and improve upon) the performance of DAL for systems governed by the combined effects of inertia and friction.

Solution

To address the challenge of reconstructing initial conditions in viscous flows, we compare the following three iterative approaches:

1. Direct Adjoint Looping (DAL): A standard optimal control technique that uses forward and backward passes to optimize the initial state by minimizing a cost function. While widely used in meteorology and data assimilation, its effectiveness for fully nonlinear deterministic fluid problems was previously untested.

2. Simple Backward Integration (SBI): A new method we propose that modifies the backward time-stepping procedure of DAL to include terms accounting for advection. Unlike DAL, SBI directly targets the forward dynamics' reversibility while maintaining numerical stability.

3. Quasi‑Reversible Method (QRM): Originally developed for linear ill-posed problems, QRM introduces artificial viscosity and evolves the system backward in time. We adapt this method to the Navier–Stokes equations to test its performance in nonlinear settings.

Results

Figure 3 from our paper (shown below) illustrates how each method reconstructs a 1D Korteweg–de Vries–Burgers (KdVB) initial condition over successive iterations. The optimal "target" initial condition is plotted in magenta. We want our "trial" initial conditions \( u_n(x, 0) \) to converge to the target in as few iterations as possible.



1. (DAL 1, top left) This case uses first-order gradient descent. It slowly shifts and amplifies the trial initial state but struggles to match the target shape after 200 iterations.

2. (DAL 2, top right) This case uses the second-order L-BFGS method. It converges faster than DAL 1, but introduces oscillatory artifacts not present in the true initial state.

3. (SBI, bottom left) steadily shifts the trial solution toward the target with smooth updates. After about 50 iterations, the match is nearly perfect.

4. (QRM, bottom right) rapidly captures the target structure within a single iteration, though small-scale oscillations remain even after 200 iterations.

Background

Magnetic fields in planets, stars, and laboratory experiments can be generated through the motion of electrically conducting fluids. In the kinematic dynamo problem, we assume the magnetic field is weak and therefore has no influence on the flow (no Lorentz force). Our goal is to determine whether some velocity field \( \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\) is capable of amplifying the magnetic field \( \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x},t)\).

Problem

The governing equations are the incompressible kinematic magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) system:

\[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0\] \[\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{u} = - \nabla p + \frac{\mathbf{\hat{z}}\times\mathbf{u}}{\rm{Ro}} + \frac{1}{\rm{Re}} \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}\] \[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0 \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{b}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{b}) + \frac{1}{\rm{Rm}} \nabla^2 \mathbf{b},\]

where we have nondimensionalized such that Ro, Re, and Rm respectively denote the viscous rotation time, viscous time, and ohmic diffusion time. We choose Ro and Re such that \( \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\) develops stable Taylor vortices which oscillate periodically in time. This Taylor-vortex flow models a rotating shear system such as Taylor–Couette flow. The central question is whether this flow sustains a fast dynamo—one whose magnetic growth rate remains \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) as \(\mathrm{Rm} \to \infty\).

Solution

We compute the leading Floquet mode of the induction operator using the power method implemented in the Dedalus pseudospectral solver:

  1. Integrate the induction equation forward from a seed magnetic field.
  2. Normalize the field and record the amplification factor.
  3. Iterate steps 1 and 2 until convergence.

This procedure allows us to compute the magnetic field's growth rate and Floquet mode.

Results

The video below shows the converged Floquet mode. The left panel is the streamwise component of the Taylor-vortex flow (\(u_y)\), and the right panel is the streamwise magnetic field of the dominant Floquet mode (\(b_y)\). Our main finding is that the unsteady Taylor-vortex dynamo is a fast dynamo: its magnetic growth rate remains positive and finite as \( \mathrm{Rm} \to \infty \).
